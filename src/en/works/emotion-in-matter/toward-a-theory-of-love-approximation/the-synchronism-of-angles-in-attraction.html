The Synchronism of Angles in Attraction

2025 · Print on canvas · 120×120 cm

We are not observing matter. We are observing the shape that matter can take.

This graph doesn't depict atoms. It depicts folds.

Every molecule shown here — a peptide, a short chain of amino acids — has an almost invisible skeleton: the backbone. It's the structure that repeats, identical, in every peptide that exists. A constant grammar, an order that doesn't distinguish one molecule from another. Like paper before it's folded, the backbone is the shared matter, not what makes each shape unique.

The difference is born from how that skeleton folds.

A sheet of paper can become a crane or a boat. The cellulose is the same; only the angles of the folds change. Yet the crane and the boat are no longer the same object: they have a different shape, a different function, a different way of existing.

Something remarkably similar happens to a molecule — but with immense biological consequences.

Along the backbone, every point can rotate within certain angles. A minimal variation is enough for the chain to take on a different geometry. And a different geometry means a different capacity to recognise the world: binding to a receptor, or brushing past it with no effect at all; triggering a process, or leaving it silent. Sometimes the distance between acting and not acting is contained within a few degrees of rotation.

This is why the graph doesn't observe atoms.

Atoms are the shared matter. Angles are the possibility of form.

It doesn't show the sculpture, but the origami that makes it possible.

Each edge doesn't represent a chemical bond. It represents a statistical dependency: if one fold changes, which others tend to change with it? Which rotations seem to answer to the same hidden rhythm? The drawing doesn't follow the chemistry of bonds, but the geometry of transformation.

The molecules involved in this phase are covered in the Method.

Naturally, not all these relations carry the same degree of certainty, and this difference isn't hidden but made explicit. For some molecules, the synchronism emerges directly from experimental data. For others, where the available evidence isn't enough, it is estimated from their conformational structure, introducing a declared margin of noise. Uncertainty isn't a flaw in the model: it's an integral part of its language, and it is shown, never disguised as certainty.

Attraction, then, can be imagined as a moment in which many folds, though belonging to different molecules with no direct bond between them, begin to answer to the same cadence — like separate sheets of paper crossed by the same current of air.

This graph tries to make exactly that instant visible.

Not the matter of attraction.

Its invisible geometry.
